The method: Recall – Spark – Learn – Apply – Prove

One five-beat structure for every lesson, theory or practical. Theory leads, practical confirms, assessment completes: each practical opens by predicting what the theory says will happen, and each theory lesson closes by naming where it will be proved in the workshop.

BeatTheory lessonPractical lesson
1. Recall
5–10 min		Closed-book retrieval on last lesson and last month: quiz, whiteboards, sort cards.Safety and standards recall, then retrieve the theory: “What should we see, and why?”
2. Spark
5–10 min		A problem before the content: a faulty component, a failed job photo, a mystery box, a customer complaint. Learners attempt or predict first.Predict: “What will happen when we do this? What could go wrong?” Learners commit to a prediction.
3. Learn
20–40 min		Learners find out via prompt cards, real components, manuals and specs, short clips. Teacher circulates and questions. Teacher input in bursts of about 5 minutes.Micro-demo with the teacher thinking aloud, learners predicting the next step, then guided practice on a job card with support fading.
4. Apply
20–40 min		Use it on a new scenario: diagnose, calculate, design, or teach it to a partner.Do it for real, then error-hunt: a deliberately flawed example to inspect, or peer-check against the standard.
5. Prove
10–15 min		Hinge question or exit check, then a practice assessment question. Teacher reads results and adjusts the next lesson.Assess against criteria: self and peer check, photo evidence, teacher sign-off, then “what would I do differently?”

Five rules for the team

  1. Learners attempt before they are told. The Spark creates the need to know.
  2. Teacher talk in short bursts, roughly five minutes at a time (a practical rule of thumb, not a research number), always followed by learner activity.
  3. One slide per beat at most. A slide is a prompt, a picture or a question, never a script.
  4. Check understanding in every beat, and change what you do next because of it.
  5. Teach the vocabulary of the trade deliberately: say it, write it, use it.

What the evidence says

The evidence does not say “never explain anything”. Rosenshine’s principles favour short, explicit teaching in small steps, with models, guided practice and constant checking. Long, passive teacher talk gives learners too little practice, so keep explanation short and get learners doing and retrieving quickly. How far learners can explore unaided is still debated, so every investigation in these lessons is guided by prompts, models and checks.

What inspectors look for now

  • Learning planned in a logical sequence, so new ideas build on prior learning and are retained.
  • Enough planned time to teach, practise, assess and close knowledge gaps.
  • Subject vocabulary taught, spoken and written.
  • Assessment used to check understanding and adapt teaching, with constructive feedback, and progress recorded accurately from each learner’s starting point.
  • At the “strong” standard, teachers handle complex topics skilfully, linking new ideas to existing knowledge so learners can retain and apply them confidently, using highly effective activities tailored to subject and learner.

Source: Ofsted FE and skills inspection toolkit, updated June 2026, used from 1 September 2026. The toolkit does not address practical or workshop teaching directly, so we evidence good practical teaching through sequencing, practice time and assessment.

Recurring weaknesses

Ofsted analysis of inspection reports, reported by TES in 2019, found weaknesses in assessment practice, target setting and feedback recurring year after year. That analysis predates the current framework, so treat it as background. There is no reliable sector-wide figure for how much “chalk and talk” there is, so our starting point is what we see in our own lessons.

What works in vocational teaching

Lucas, Spencer and Claxton’s framework of vocational pedagogy includes learning by watching and imitating, practising, feedback, conversation, and learning by teaching and helping others. Bill Lucas also challenges the myth that practical learning involves only lower-order thinking.

Explain, practise, check

  • Start with a short review of prior learning (retrieval, not re-telling).
  • Present new material in small steps with practice after each step.
  • Provide models and worked examples.
  • Guide practice, check understanding often, aim for a high success rate and scaffold hard tasks.
  • Then require independent practice, and review weekly and monthly.

Prompts work best as scaffolds with models and checks, which is why each investigation in these lessons is paired with short teaching, worked examples and a check on understanding.

Independent learning

The EEF rates metacognition and self-regulation as high-impact and low-cost. Its guidance for 16–19 education names three promising approaches to independent learning: metacognition and self-regulation, feedback (showing learners what good work looks like and what concrete steps will help them improve), and collaborative learning. Teach learners to plan, monitor and evaluate their own work, and model your own thinking aloud.

Quick observation look-fors

  • Did learners attempt something before being told?
  • Was prior learning retrieved without notes?
  • Who was talking, and for how long, at each point?
  • Was the practical linked back to the theory, and the theory forward to the practical?
  • What did the teacher change because of what they found out?
  • Could every learner say what they learned and how they know?

Suggested roll-out

  1. Share the five beats with the team and agree the non-negotiables (the rules above).
  2. Each trade team builds one theory and one practical lesson in the structure, using their own scheme of work.
  3. Peer-observe with the look-fors, then refine.
  4. Build the structure into lesson plan templates and the learning walk record.

Inclusion & SEND

Inclusion runs through every lesson. This page sets out what the Ofsted toolkit expects of teaching and shows how to build adjustments into each of the five beats, so they are planned in, not added afterwards.

Scope: the toolkit grades inclusion at whole-provider level. This page covers what teachers do in lessons. Identifying needs, EHC plans, funding and access arrangements sit with the SEND and learning support team, so ask them early.

What the toolkit expects

  • Teachers know how to reduce barriers to learning and promote wellbeing.
  • Teachers use evidence to adapt teaching for disadvantaged learners, learners with SEND or high needs, looked-after learners and others facing barriers.
  • Where appropriate, teachers work with specialists, employers and families to make reasonable adjustments and support progression.
  • At the expected standard, the curriculum is accessible, adjustments match learners’ needs, and learners with high needs follow planned outcomes (such as their EHC plan targets) and are prepared for their next steps.
  • Barriers are addressed without lowering expectations. A graduated approach (assess, plan, do, review) generally meets needs.
  • At the strong standard, support is tailored and coordinated, it is reviewed systematically, and it is withdrawn when no longer needed so that learners become more independent.
  • Warning signs: adjustments that are not well matched to needs (“needs attention”), or specific needs not identified and adjustments that are inadequate (“urgent improvement”).

Source: Ofsted FE and skills inspection toolkit, Inclusion and Curriculum, teaching and training sections.

Build it into the five beats

The EEF describes adaptive teaching in four steps: anticipate barriers, plan to address them, use assessment to find out what learners have learned, and make adaptations in the moment. Its five-a-day approaches for learners with SEND are flexible grouping, cognitive and metacognitive strategies, explicit instruction, using technology, and scaffolding. They support all learners, not only those with SEND.

BeatInclusive practice, essential for some and helpful for all
1. RecallEvery learner answers (mini whiteboards, not hands up). Allow thinking time. Provide a word bank for key vocabulary. Keep it low stakes so that mistakes are normal.
2. SparkUse a real object, photo or short clip with a question in plain words. Pre-teach any vocabulary the Spark relies on. Let learners respond by speaking, drawing or writing.
3. LearnExplicit instruction in small steps: teacher modelling and thinking aloud, then guided practice. Provide scaffolds such as step cards, photo job cards and sentence starters, and withdraw them gradually as confidence grows. Use temporary groupings based on shared needs, not fixed ability groups. Keep one idea in focus at a time.
4. ApplySame outcome for everyone, different levels of support: a full worked example, a partly completed one, or an independent task. Use technology where it helps, such as read-aloud, voice notes and photo evidence.
5. ProveCheck the criteria first. Where the assessment allows, learners can show what they know in different ways, such as a demonstration, an oral explanation or photos. Before changing how anyone is assessed, check the awarding body’s rules on reasonable adjustments and access arrangements with the learning support team.
Practical lessonsAgree adjustments before the session and check that the risk assessment reflects them. Use job cards with photos and a clear sequence. Adjust the support, never the safety standard.

Before, during and after the lesson

Before

  • Check learner profiles and EHC plan targets with the learning support team.
  • Plan one or two adjustments for each beat.
  • Prepare scaffolds in advance: step cards, word banks, photo job cards.

During and after

  • Check every learner’s understanding in every beat and adapt in the moment.
  • Withdraw support when it is no longer needed.
  • Record what worked, and tell learning support early if you have a concern.

Disadvantaged learners and other barriers

The toolkit also names disadvantaged learners, young carers, looked-after learners and those without level 2 English or maths. It expects staff to notice disengagement or declining wellbeing and to offer support sensitively.

  • Teach and use vocabulary deliberately, spoken and written.
  • Embed English and maths in the trade, for example calculating tolerances, reading data sheets and writing job reports.
  • Notice changes such as lateness, withdrawal or a drop in quality of work, and pass concerns on through your safeguarding and pastoral routes. Follow your college’s safeguarding procedures for any concern about a learner’s welfare.

Quick observation look-fors

  • Could every learner get started on the task?
  • Were adjustments matched to individuals, not the same for everyone?
  • Were expectations still high?
  • Did scaffolds fade as learners gained confidence?
  • Did the teacher change what they did after checking understanding?
Evidence note: the EEF’s SEND guidance was written for mainstream schools and its evidence review focused on pupils aged 5–16. It does not address post-16 settings, so treat it as a sound starting point and apply professional judgement, working with your learning support team.

Behaviour & attendance

Unmissable lessons help attendance and behaviour, but they do not replace clear expectations and consistent follow-up. This page sets out what the toolkit expects and what you can build into every session.

Scope: attendance thresholds, absence procedures and sanctions are for leaders and the attendance and pastoral teams. This page covers what teachers can do in lessons, and it follows your college’s own policies.

What the toolkit expects

These points come from the Participation and development part of the toolkit.

  • Welcoming environments where bullying, discrimination, harassment, victimisation, violence and derogatory language are not tolerated.
  • Support for regular attendance and enthusiastic participation, with high standards and help to improve when needed.
  • Appropriate behaviour in study and at work, dealt with swiftly and consistently. Learners behave professionally where relevant, including professional standards for apprentices.
  • At the expected standard, leaders set high expectations, identify the reasons for poor attendance and help learners re-engage, and inappropriate behaviour is addressed consistently and promptly.
  • At the strong standard, high attendance and participation are routinely achieved by all groups.
  • Warning signs: attendance work is inconsistent so improvement for some groups is limited, or staff lack the skills to manage poor behaviour that disrupts learning (“needs attention”). Low attendance with barriers not tackled is “urgent improvement”.

Source: Ofsted FE and skills inspection toolkit, Participation and development section.

Behaviour: six evidence-informed actions

The EEF’s behaviour guidance has six recommendations. Here is what each can look like in a workshop or classroom.

EEF recommendationWhat it can look like in your workshop or classroom (suggested)
1. Know and understand your learners and their influencesLearn each learner’s context, starting point and barriers, using starting point assessments and learning support information. Make sure every learner has a supportive relationship with a member of staff.
2. Teach learning behaviours alongside managing misbehaviourExplicitly teach the behaviours that support learning: attention, perseverance, asking for help and working safely. Teach workshop routines in the first week, then revisit them.
3. Use classroom management strategiesPlan groupings and positions, signal the start and end of tasks, move around the room and use quiet, private reminders. Use rewards as part of a wider strategy, not on their own.
4. Use simple approaches as part of your regular routineGreet learners at the door, give specific praise and start every session the same way, with an entry task on the board (the Recall beat). Keep expectations clear.
5. Use targeted approaches for individualsFor learners who need more, agree short personal targets and review them regularly with the learner. The EEF found good evidence for personalised approaches such as daily report cards. Do not lower expectations.
6. Consistency is keyEveryone in the team applies the same expectations and responses. Agree them together, use shared wording, and plan how changes will be embedded over time.

Professional standards matter in every trade. Teach and model punctuality, PPE, respectful language, working safely and tidying up, and treat them as part of the standard of the job, not as extras.

How the five-beat structure helps

  • Recall: an entry task on the board means learners start straight away and the room settles.
  • Spark: curiosity gains attention without needing control.
  • Learn: short teacher input with one instruction at a time keeps everyone busy and clear.
  • Apply: clear roles in pairs and groups, and stage checks, keep practical work on track.
  • Prove: a clear close, with tidying, review and a preview of the next session.

These are practical suggestions that fit the evidence, not tested claims about this structure.

Evidence note: the EEF behaviour guidance was written for primary and secondary schools and reviewed evidence for pupils aged 4 to 18. Consistent application across the whole team is the common theme.

Attendance: what the evidence says

  • The EEF describes attendance as a significant challenge in 16–19 education, with average attendance lower in colleges than in secondary schools, especially in English and maths. It also notes there is little evidence on which strategies improve attendance.
  • The Children’s Commissioner’s September 2026 report used Spring term 2025/26 data from colleges in England. Average attendance was 84.8% for 16-year-olds and 83.4% for 17-year-olds, against 91.6% in secondary schools. About 52% were below 90%, and about 5.9% attended under half their sessions. English and maths resit attendance was lower, at around 75%.
  • Severe absence was higher among learners eligible for free school meals, those with an EHC plan, looked-after learners, and Level 1 and Entry Level learners. These are associations, not proven causes. Colleges do not share a common definition of attendance, and the national figures are estimated from the colleges that responded.
  • Colleges cited mental health, poverty, transport costs and availability, caring responsibilities, part-time work, difficult transitions and poor earlier experiences of education as causes.
  • Approaches colleges use or report as effective include early identification through tracking and thresholds, personalised support for the underlying barrier, partnerships with families, schools and agencies, and building a sense of belonging through enrichment and pastoral support. Several of these are self-reported.

The first six weeks

Department for Education case studies of colleges (January 2023) on early retention describe the first six weeks as the time when learners are most receptive and most vulnerable. They report:

  • Setting clear expectations on attendance, deadlines and conduct early.
  • Staff taking time to understand learners’ aspirations and needs: “connect before correct”.
  • Early personal support, with a named person learners can contact.
  • A risk rating around week 5 and regular case conferences for the at-risk group.
  • Short assessments every two to four weeks to celebrate achievement and guide next steps.
  • Delaying overly demanding tasks until support is in place.
  • A named contact to move a learner to a more suitable programme rather than leave (“Swap, don’t drop”).
  • Simple monitoring systems, which were more likely to be used consistently than complex ones.

These are case-study findings, not controlled trials.

What teachers can build into lessons

These are suggested practices that fit the evidence above. The evidence for individual tactics is limited, so check what works with your own learners.

  • Make the first six weeks count. Set expectations early, plan early wins such as finishing a real piece of work, and use short checks every few weeks.
  • Give every session a reason to attend. Use a Spark that creates curiosity, a practical outcome learners want to finish, and end by naming next session’s job.
  • Connect before you correct. Greet learners, ask about absence without blame, find the reason, then agree the next step.
  • Make catching up easy. Share the Recall questions, key vocabulary and photo job card for missed sessions so that absence does not snowball.
  • Notice patterns early and pass them on. Look for the same day or session being missed, lateness and quiet withdrawal. Record attendance accurately using your college’s codes and refer to the attendance or pastoral team.
  • Signpost support for barriers such as transport, work, caring and health before reaching for sanctions.
  • Recognise good attendance and progress, not only the absence.

Quick observation look-fors

  • Was the entry routine in place, and did learners start straight away?
  • Were expectations applied calmly and consistently?
  • Did the teacher greet learners and follow up absence or lateness?
  • Were learners given a reason to come back next session?

Automotive

Timings assume a 2.5-hour session. For a shorter session, scale them down and keep the order.

Why this works here: Brakes, steering and electrics are safety-critical, so learners must explain why, not just follow steps. Fault-finding is naturally an investigation, so lessons can start with a symptom instead of a definition.

Vocabulary to teach: caliper, master cylinder, hydraulic, incompressible, hygroscopic, disc run-out, wear limit, torque

Theory Why does the brake pedal work? Hydraulic braking

Learning outcome: By the end, learners can explain how force is multiplied and transmitted in a hydraulic brake system, and why air or water in the fluid is a problem.

Activity bank

  • Fault forensics: a vehicle with a safe, planted fault; learners investigate and justify the cause.
  • Mistake museum: glazed pads, scored discs, leaking seals; what went wrong and how to prevent it.
  • Mystery part tray: identify and explain each part by touch and questions only.
  • Beat your best: measure a reference disc repeatedly and improve how close your readings get to the true value.
  • Service adviser role-play: explain a fault to a customer in plain English.
  • Predict, observe, explain: battery voltage under load.

Misconceptions to retrieve and correct

  • Air compresses; brake fluid does not. That is why air makes the pedal spongy.
  • Brake fluid absorbs moisture over time, which lowers its boiling point; it does not last forever.
  • A worn pad is a safety issue, not just a comfort or noise issue.

Inclusion, behaviour and attendance ideas

Ideas to consider. Agree adjustments with your learning support team and keep risk assessments up to date. See also Inclusion & SEND and Behaviour & attendance.

  • Inclusion: Provide a step-by-step job card with photos for the inspection sequence and a glossary of parts. Agree with learning support whether a learner needs extra time, a buddy or an adapted way to take measurements.
  • Behaviour: Teach the workshop routine explicitly in the first week: PPE on at the door, phones away at the vehicle, tools returned to the board. Greet learners at the workshop door and start every session with the same Recall task.
  • Attendance: End each theory session by naming the job on the vehicle that comes next, such as “next week you measure real discs”, so a missed session has a visible cost. Give absent learners the Recall questions and a photo job card to catch up.

Engineering

Timings assume a 2.5-hour session. For a shorter session, scale them down and keep the order.

Why this works here: Precision work depends on measurement and drawings becoming automatic, which is a strong case for daily retrieval. Judging a part against a drawing suits peer inspection and blind swaps.

Vocabulary to teach: tolerance, upper and lower limit, clearance, interference, datum, vernier, micrometer, go/no-go

Theory Limits, tolerances and fits

Learning outcome: By the end, learners can read a dimension with a tolerance, calculate its upper and lower limits, and decide whether a measured part passes.

Activity bank

  • Spot the error: drawings with seeded mistakes.
  • Fit or fail bench: shafts and bores to sort and explain.
  • Beat your best: how close can you get to the true value measuring a reference piece?
  • Expert envelopes: each group masters one process, then groups combine to plan a whole part.
  • Employer brief: a batch of ten parts with reject criteria.
  • Blind swap inspection.

Misconceptions to retrieve and correct

  • Tolerance is allowed variation, not a mistake.
  • A smaller shaft is not automatically acceptable; it may be too loose for the fit.
  • Reading a vernier: take the whole millimetres from the main scale, then add the fraction from the vernier line that lines up with a main-scale line.

Inclusion, behaviour and attendance ideas

Ideas to consider. Agree adjustments with your learning support team and keep risk assessments up to date. See also Inclusion & SEND and Behaviour & attendance.

  • Inclusion: Offer a large-scale or digital reading option for measuring instruments where it suits the learner, plus a worked example of each calculation.
  • Behaviour: Treat machine and workshop safety routines as non-negotiable. Teach them as explicit steps, recheck them at the start of every session and use a short “ready to start” checklist before machinery is used.
  • Attendance: Keep the part learners are making and their stage inspection record on show so they can see their progress and want to finish it.

Painting & Decorating

Timings assume a 2.5-hour session. For a shorter session, scale them down and keep the order.

Why this works here: Most of the quality is in the preparation, and failures only show up later. Starting a lesson with a failed finish gives learners a real reason to learn preparation.

Vocabulary to teach: substrate, key, primer, undercoat, adhesion, blistering, flaking, cutting in, touch-dry and cured

Theory Why paint fails: preparation and adhesion

Learning outcome: By the end, learners can identify common causes of paint failure and choose the preparation needed for a given surface.

BeatMinsWhat learners doPrompt or resourceHow I check understanding

Plumbing

Timings assume a 2.5-hour session. For a shorter session, scale them down and keep the order.

Why this works here: A leaking joint is a visible, memorable failure, so lessons can begin with a joint that failed. The physics behind joints (capillary action, pressure) can be explored with simple apparatus before it is named, as long as prompts guide the learners.

Electrical Installation

Timings assume a 2.5-hour session. For a shorter session, scale them down and keep the order.

Why this works here: Electricity is invisible, so predicting and then measuring is the heart of learning it. Safe working has to be taught explicitly every lesson, and fault-finding gives a strong reason to understand circuits.

Vocabulary to teach: voltage, current, resistance, series, parallel, open circuit, continuity, safe isolation

Theory Ohm’s law, series and parallel circuits

Learning outcome: By the end, learners can calculate voltage, current and resistance in simple series and parallel circuits and predict meter readings.

Activity bank

  • Fault-finding boards with planted faults.
  • Predict, observe, explain on a simple circuit.
  • Mystery box: deduce the circuit inside using only a meter.
  • Beat your best: accuracy of meter readings against a known value.
  • Mistake museum: photos of poor terminations and what could happen.
  • Peer inspector with a termination checklist.

Misconceptions to retrieve and correct

  • Current is not used up by a bulb; it is the same all the way round a series circuit.
  • More resistance means less current at the same voltage, not more.
  • A meter used on the wrong setting or connected the wrong way can give false readings or be damaged.

Inclusion, behaviour and attendance ideas

Ideas to consider. Agree adjustments with your learning support team and keep risk assessments up to date. See also Inclusion & SEND and Behaviour & attendance.

  • Inclusion: Use laminated photo step cards for meter set-up. Where colour identification is part of the assessment, check the awarding body’s guidance.
  • Behaviour: Teach low-voltage working and safe isolation as explicit routines and recheck them in every lesson.
  • Attendance: Predict-then-measure tasks give a reason to be there: the readings only exist if learners build the circuit.

Carpentry

Timings assume a 2.5-hour session. For a shorter session, scale them down and keep the order.

Why this works here: Timber behaves differently depending on moisture and grain, which learners can discover by measuring and observing. Cutting accurately to a line is a skill that improves fastest with demonstration, practice and quick feedback.

Activity bank

  • Joint autopsy: take apart failed joints and find the cause.
  • Mistake museum: joints with gaps, wrong waste side, split timber.
  • Mystery timber: identify by grain, weight and smell.
  • Beat your best: sawing accuracy along a line over repeated attempts.
  • Real brief: cut list and material calculation for a small job.

  • Predict, observe, explain: moisture and movement.

Misconceptions to retrieve and correct

  • Timber does not stop moving once cut; it keeps responding to moisture.
  • Sawing on the line is not enough; saw on the waste side so the marked line remains.
  • A moisture reading depends on where and how the timber was stored.

Inclusion, behaviour and attendance ideas

Ideas to consider. Agree adjustments with your learning support team and keep risk assessments up to date. See also Inclusion & SEND and Behaviour & attendance.

  • Inclusion: Offer marking aids such as guides, clamps and jigs, following the learning support team’s advice, and keep a model joint on the bench to compare against.
  • Behaviour: Hand tool routines: tools out and back on the board, and a clear rule on sharp tools. Greet learners at the workshop door and start with the same Recall task.
  • Attendance: A small project learners keep, such as a joint or box, builds ownership. Show the sequence clearly so a learner who misses a session knows exactly what to catch up.
